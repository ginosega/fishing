## Overview

Spring is a transition season. The calendar matters less than the direction and stability of the water temperature: several mild days can move fish dramatically, while a cold front can temporarily push them back toward the last reliable depth or cover.

For bass, spring is the migration from winter habitat to spawning areas and then away from the beds again. For trout, cool water opens a wide band of comfortable habitat, stocked rainbows can be abundant in lowland lakes, and resident trout often spend more time near shore or higher in the water column than they will in summer.

Use this page for the seasonal question: **where should I look first today, and how should I adjust my presentation for spring?** For the underlying habitat logic, see [Bass Behavior and Habitat](kb://bass-behavior-and-habitat) and [Trout Behavior and Habitat](kb://trout-behavior-and-habitat). For lure mechanics and rigging, see [Bass Fishing Techniques](kb://bass-fishing-techniques) and [Trout Fishing Techniques](kb://technique-trout-fishing).

## Bass: Where to Find Them

Spring bass location is best understood as a route rather than a destination:

**wintering water → staging structure → spawning area → first postspawn holding cover/structure**.

Fish do not all move at once, so a lake can hold bass in several phases on the same day.

### Early Spring / Cold Prespawn

When the lake is still cold, start one step away from the eventual spawning water rather than immediately fishing the shallowest flat.

High-percentage areas include:

- the first major break outside a protected flat or cove;
- secondary points leading toward spawning water;
- rock-to-gravel, rock-to-sand, or hard-bottom transitions;
- steep banks and channel swings;
- outside weed edges and the first emerging green vegetation;
- deeper dock ends close to a migration route;
- smallmouth-oriented rock, gravel, points, and offshore structure with a nearby shallow destination.

A few degrees of localized warming can matter. Protected north-facing exposure is not automatically best; instead look for **sun, protection from cold wind, darker bottom, shallow water, and nearby depth** acting together. A pocket that warms faster than the main lake can temporarily hold far more active fish.

### Warming Prespawn

As warming becomes more consistent, bass spread farther along the route toward spawning areas. This is when wind and forage can turn a staging area into an active feeding area.

Check:

- windward points and banks close to spawning coves;
- emerging weed lines;
- flats with a nearby ditch, channel, or drop;
- docks along a depth transition;
- rock/gravel shelves for smallmouth;
- wood, grass, and protected pockets for largemouth;
- any place holding visible baitfish or panfish.

Do not assume every fish is shallow just because one warm afternoon produced shallow activity. After a cold night or front, the same fish may simply slide from the flat to the first break or from the face of a dock to its deepest pilings.

### Spawn

Spawning bass favor protected areas with suitable bottom and enough stability to maintain a nest.

Typical starting habitat:

- gravel, sand, or other firm bottom;
- protected pockets and coves;
- shallow zones with quick access to a deeper staging area;
- rock/gravel for smallmouth, often somewhat deeper or more exposed;
- vegetation edges, wood, docks, and sheltered shallow cover for largemouth where suitable bottom exists.

Visible bed fish are vulnerable. Handle fish quickly and consider avoiding repeated targeting of the same occupied nest.

### Postspawn

Postspawn is not a single pattern. Some fish remain shallow, some recover on the first break, and others begin moving toward summer habitat.

Look for:

- shade and docks near spawning areas;
- the first drop outside the flat;
- healthy vegetation and outside weed edges;
- fry or juvenile forage;
- points and rock for smallmouth;
- wood and grass for largemouth;
- low-light surface activity as feeding resumes.

## Bass: How to Catch Them

### Cold Water: Slow and Precise

When fish are still close to winter behavior, use presentations that can remain in the strike zone.

- **Ned rig:** drag, dead-stick, or make tiny hops around rock and gravel.
- **Drop shot:** hold a small bait at fish depth around breaks and deep structure.
- **Suspending jerkbait:** use long pauses; shorten them only after fish prove willing to chase.
- **Small swimbait:** slow-roll at the depth of suspended or staging fish.
- **Finesse jig:** crawl through rock or isolated cover.

The productive change is often **slower cadence or better depth control**, not a different color.

### Warming Prespawn: Search, Then Follow Up

When fish begin roaming, cover water with a lure that matches the habitat, then slow down after you get evidence of fish.

Good search choices:

- jerkbait over points and breaks;
- swimbait around baitfish and transition zones;
- crankbait deflected off rock or hard bottom;
- spinnerbait on windy banks and around wood;
- chatterbait through emerging grass or stained water.

After a bite, follower, or missed strike, follow with a Ned rig, drop shot, wacky worm, or jig before leaving the area.

### Spawn / Postspawn: Target Fishing

For fish around shallow cover, beds, docks, or the first break:

- wacky worm or weightless plastic for a slow fall beside targets;
- jig around wood, docks, rock, and edges;
- small swimbait for roaming postspawn fish;
- [Topwater Fishing](kb://topwater-fishing) during low light once fish are willing to feed upward;
- spinnerbait/chatterbait when wind, stain, or grass favors a moving presentation.

## Trout: Where to Find Them

Spring is often the broadest lowland-lake trout window because cool water lets fish use shoreline and upper-water habitat that becomes less comfortable in summer.

Start with:

- shoreline cruising lanes;
- points and cove mouths;
- creek mouths and other inflows;
- wind-blown banks that collect insects or plankton;
- the upper water column over deeper water;
- weed edges and shoreline breaks;
- visible rises or repeated surface dimples;
- recently stocked areas when current WDFW plant information indicates fresh fish.

For resident coastal cutthroat, shoreline structure, submerged wood, breaks, and forage remain important even when fish are relatively shallow. For recently stocked rainbows, open-water roaming and loose schools can matter more than classic structure at first.

### The Daily Spring Shift

Cold mornings can fish differently from sunny afternoons. A bank or shallow pocket that looks empty at first light may become productive after several hours of warming.

Use light and wind as additional clues:

- low light or chop can keep trout high and shallow longer;
- bright midday can shift fish toward deeper water, shade, or a steeper break;
- a mild wind can concentrate food against one shoreline;
- a cold front can reduce shallow activity without eliminating fish from the broader area.

## Trout: How to Catch Them

### Active Casting

Use casting lures when trout are roaming or visibly feeding.

- **Inline spinner:** retrieve only fast enough to keep the blade working; count it down when fish are below the surface.
- **Spoon:** excellent for distance and covering multiple depths; add pauses or speed changes when fish follow.
- **Small crankbait/jerkbait:** useful when trout are feeding on minnows or are willing to chase.

Fan-cast before abandoning a spot. Change **depth and angle** before cycling through many similar colors.

### Still Fishing from Bank

Use a [Bobber Rig](kb://technique-bobber-rig) when fish are shallow or suspended and a [Slip Sinker Rig](kb://technique-slip-sinker-rig) when you need to reach deeper water or present floating bait above bottom vegetation/debris.

Do not leave an unproductive bait in one place indefinitely. Change casting distance, leader length, depth, or location until you intersect fish.

### Kayak Trolling

Spring trolling is effective because trout can be scattered through open water.

- Start relatively shallow when fish are in the upper water column.
- Use enough weight to control depth, not more than necessary.
- Make gentle turns so one lure speeds/rises while the other slows/falls.
- After a strike, repeat the same depth, speed, and route before changing gear.
- If sonar shows fish at a consistent depth, target that depth even when the bottom is much deeper.

## Spring Conditions That Matter Most

### Warming Trend vs. One Warm Day

A multi-day warming trend is more reliable than a single warm afternoon. Bass especially may advance toward shallow water after stable warming and retreat a short distance after a strong cold front.

### Wind

Moderate wind can improve both fisheries by concentrating forage and reducing visibility. For bass, prioritize wind-blown points, weed edges, and banks. For trout, look for shorelines where insects, plankton, or other food are accumulating.

### Water Clarity

- Clear water: longer casts, natural colors, subtler profiles, and greater attention to depth/shade.
- Light stain: moving baits become easier for bass to ambush; trout may tolerate a brighter or more visible lure.
- Heavy stain: bass often orient tighter to cover and respond to vibration/contrast; trout fishing may be better near cleaner inflow edges or clearer adjacent water.

## A Practical Spring Trip Plan

1. **Check the water and recent weather.** Note temperature trend, clarity, wind, and whether a cold front just passed.
2. **For bass, identify the spawning destination and fish the route toward it.** Start at the first break/point and move shallower only as evidence supports it.
3. **For trout, check stocking information and scan the upper water column/shoreline first.** Do not automatically start deep in cool water.
4. **Use a search presentation.** Jerkbait/swimbait/spinnerbait for bass; spoon/spinner for trout.
5. **Slow down where you get evidence.** Finesse bass presentations or a controlled trout depth often converts a clue into multiple fish.
6. **Recheck productive shallow water later in the day.** Spring conditions can change quickly enough that a morning dead zone becomes an afternoon feeding area.
