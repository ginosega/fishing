## Overview

This page is about **finding trout**. It complements [Trout Fishing Techniques](kb://technique-trout-fishing), which focuses on choosing and working the right presentation after you have found likely fish. For the way location and presentation change through the year, use [Spring Fishing](kb://spring-fishing), [Summer Fishing](kb://summer-fishing), [Fall Fishing](kb://fall-fishing), and [Winter Fishing](kb://winter-fishing).

For the trout fisheries represented in this library, location is driven mainly by five things:

1. **Temperature:** trout are cold-water fish, so warming water often pushes them deeper, toward inflows, or into other thermal refuges.
2. **Dissolved oxygen:** deep water is useful only if it still contains enough oxygen.
3. **Food:** insects, zooplankton, crustaceans, baitfish, and other prey can concentrate trout more strongly than structure alone.
4. **Light and security:** trout often feed shallower in low light and move deeper or closer to cover in bright conditions.
5. **Current and water movement:** inflows, outlets, wind, current seams, and vertical mixing can concentrate both food and comfortable water.

Use those variables together. A cool creek mouth with current and food is much more promising than simply “deep water,” and a shallow wind-blown shoreline can outfish the middle of a lake when cool water and forage make it comfortable.

The two primary trout species in this library are [Rainbow Trout](kb://species-rainbow-trout) and [Coastal Cutthroat Trout](kb://species-coastal-cutthroat-trout).

| Trout | Useful Starting Tendency |
| --- | --- |
| Rainbow trout | In stocked lowland lakes, often roam open water and shoreline zones; as water warms, increasingly use deeper/cooler water. In streams, use pools, runs, seams, and current breaks. |
| Coastal cutthroat trout | Opportunistic and often structure/shoreline oriented; commonly use submerged debris, drop-offs, deep pools, and lake edges, but move deeper/offshore during warm summer periods. |

Treat these as starting tendencies rather than rules. Trout move quickly when food, light, temperature, or oxygen changes.

## The Comfort Zone: Temperature and Oxygen

### Temperature

Temperature is one of the strongest location clues for trout.

A useful rule is:

- **Cool water:** trout can use shallow water, shoreline areas, and the upper water column more freely.
- **Warming water:** begin checking deeper water, shaded areas, inflows, springs, creek mouths, and steeper breaks.
- **Hot surface water:** do not assume trout are simply “on the bottom.” Find the depth where **cool-enough water, oxygen, and food overlap**.

Habitat literature for rainbow trout commonly identifies water below about **64°F / 18°C** as preferred adult lake habitat, with stream habitat often best in roughly the 50s to low 60s°F. See the [U.S. Forest Service Rainbow Trout habitat profile](https://www.fs.usda.gov/Internet/FSE_DOCUMENTS/stelprdb5206802.pdf). These are useful search clues, not hard behavioral cutoffs; strain, acclimation, food, oxygen, and local conditions all matter.

### Dissolved Oxygen and the Warm-Water Squeeze

As lakes stratify, the upper layer can become too warm while deep water can lose oxygen. Trout may become compressed into a relatively narrow band where both temperature and oxygen are acceptable.

That means:

- deeper is not automatically better;
- fish and bait repeatedly appearing at the same depth on sonar may mark the usable layer;
- the thermocline can be an important starting depth;
- a point, hump, or shoreline break intersecting that depth can be especially productive;
- inflows and springs can create local exceptions.

If sonar shows fish concentrated at one depth across several parts of a lake, treat that **depth itself as the pattern**.

## How Trout Use Lakes

### Shorelines, Breaks, and Deep-Water Access

In cool conditions, trout may cruise surprisingly close to shore. High-percentage areas include:

- points that extend toward deep water;
- creek mouths and inflows;
- cove mouths;
- shoreline drop-offs;
- submerged wood and debris;
- weed edges near open water;
- steep shaded banks;
- docks and other shade where legal to fish;
- the edge of a flat where it falls into deeper water.

A shoreline with quick access to depth is more reliable through changing conditions than a broad shallow flat with no nearby refuge.

### Inflows, Outlets, and Springs

Moving water can concentrate trout because it may provide some combination of cooler temperature, oxygen, drifting food, and a travel route.

Check:

- creek mouths;
- small tributary inflows;
- spring-fed areas;
- culverts or channels that create flow;
- outlets and current seams;
- the edge between moving and still water.

After rain, an inflow can become especially interesting if it adds cool water and food without becoming excessively muddy or turbulent.

### Wind

Light to moderate wind can improve lake fishing by mixing the surface, reducing light penetration, and pushing plankton or insects toward a shoreline.

On windy days, check:

- wind-blown banks and points;
- the downwind end of a small lake;
- shorelines where insects or surface debris are accumulating;
- the edge between wind-churned and protected water.

For trout, wind is most useful when it improves **food and comfort**. If it simply muddies shallow water or makes presentation control poor, move to a nearby transition rather than insisting on the windiest bank.

### Forage and Feeding Clues

Trout are opportunistic. Depending on size, species, lake, and conditions, important food can include:

- aquatic insects and emerging insects;
- terrestrial insects blown or falling into the water;
- zooplankton;
- leeches and other invertebrates;
- crustaceans;
- fish eggs;
- minnows and juvenile fish.

Watch for:

- surface dimples or repeated rises;
- birds or swallows feeding over one area;
- insect activity;
- bait or small fish on sonar;
- trout following but not striking a lure;
- repeated fish marks at the same depth.

When trout are visibly rising, start by treating the **feeding zone** as more important than bottom structure. When surface activity disappears, use sonar and temperature/depth clues to find where the food moved.

## Light and Daily Movement

Trout often use shallower water or the upper water column during lower light and move deeper during bright periods.

Good shallow windows commonly include:

- first light;
- evening;
- overcast periods;
- wind-chopped conditions;
- active insect hatches.

Bright midday usually increases the value of:

- depth;
- shade;
- submerged cover;
- steep banks;
- deeper weed edges;
- the thermocline or other comfortable depth band.

Lake Washington research on wild coastal cutthroat found a particularly useful pattern: fish were generally **deeper during summer stratification, shallower during winter mixing, and shallower at night than during the day**. That is a strong reminder that trout location can shift vertically without the fish leaving the broader area.

## Stocked Rainbow Trout vs. Resident Trout

Washington's lowland-lake rainbow fishery includes many hatchery-stocked fish. Stocking history can therefore be a legitimate location clue.

For recently stocked rainbow trout:

- check the current stocking report before a trip;
- begin by checking accessible shoreline/open-water areas and the upper water column;
- watch for schools of cruising fish rather than assuming they are already relating tightly to natural structure;
- as fish acclimate and natural food becomes more important, expect location to be governed increasingly by temperature, forage, and lake structure.

Resident trout and fish grown from fry/fingerling plants have had more time to learn the lake's natural food and habitat, so forage and environmental patterns become more important than the stocking location itself.

Current Washington stocking information:
- [WDFW Statewide Hatchery Trout and Kokanee Stocking Plan](https://wdfw.wa.gov/fishing/reports/stocking/statewide)
- [WDFW Catchable Trout Plant Reports](https://wdfw.wa.gov/fishing/reports/stocking/trout-plants)

## Streams and Rivers

In moving water, trout try to maximize food while minimizing the energy required to hold position.

Think in terms of **feeding lanes beside resting water**.

High-percentage locations include:

- the downstream side of boulders;
- seams between fast and slow current;
- heads and tails of pools;
- undercut banks;
- deep pockets beside riffles;
- submerged wood;
- shaded banks;
- deeper runs;
- places where a tributary enters;
- soft water immediately beside the main flow.

A trout may sit in slower water and move only a short distance into faster current to intercept food. The best position often combines **nearby current carrying food + a low-energy holding spot + depth or cover**.

During warm, low-flow periods, prioritize the coolest available water and avoid unnecessarily stressing trout in marginal temperatures. In cold conditions, deeper pools and softer current often become more important because fish can conserve energy.

## How Seasons Reweight These Variables

The same habitat rules apply all year, but temperature, lake stratification, stocking, forage, and light change which rule matters most.

## Finding a Repeatable Trout Pattern

After a bite, follower, or cluster of sonar marks, record:

- depth;
- water temperature if available;
- surface vs. suspended vs. bottom position;
- nearby break or structure;
- shade/light;
- wind exposure;
- current/inflow;
- bait or insect activity;
- time of day;
- whether the fish was a recently stocked rainbow or a resident species if known.

Then test another area with the **same combination**.

Examples:

- If trout are suspended at 18 feet over 35 feet of water, troll other areas at 18 feet instead of following the bottom.
- If cutthroat are on steep shaded shorelines with submerged wood, search for more shoreline with that combination.
- If rainbows are cruising the top few feet after a recent plant, do not abandon that depth just because the lake is much deeper.
- If evening fish rise over a point where insects are active, revisit that point at similar light rather than assuming the fish live there all day.

The goal is to understand **why trout are using that depth and area right now**, then reproduce the conditions.
