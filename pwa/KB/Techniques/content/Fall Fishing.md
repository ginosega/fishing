## Overview

Fall is a cooling transition rather than a single pattern. Early fall can still behave like summer, mid-fall often expands shallow feeding opportunities, and late fall gradually pulls fish toward winter habitat. The common thread is that **forage movement becomes unusually important**.

For bass, cooling water and dying vegetation redistribute both cover and prey. Bass often become more mobile, use wind-blown banks and points, and follow baitfish into coves, creek arms, flats, and other feeding areas before eventually sliding back toward deeper wintering structure. For trout, cooling surface water expands comfortable habitat and can bring fish back toward shore and higher in the water column after the summer deep-water period.

Use this page for the seasonal question: **where is the food moving as the lake cools, and how aggressive should the presentation be at this stage of fall?** For enduring habitat logic, see [Bass Behavior and Habitat](kb://bass-behavior-and-habitat) and [Trout Behavior and Habitat](kb://trout-behavior-and-habitat). For lure mechanics and rigging, see [Bass Fishing Techniques](kb://bass-fishing-techniques) and [Trout Fishing Techniques](kb://technique-trout-fishing).

## Bass: Where to Find Them

### Early Fall: Summer Pattern with More Movement

When surface temperatures are still warm, start with the summer pattern rather than forcing a fall cliché.

Check:

- deep shade and healthy vegetation for largemouth;
- rock, points, humps, and offshore structure for smallmouth;
- early/late shallow feeding zones;
- the outside edge of grass where bait gathers;
- wind-blown banks and points;
- open-water bait schools.

The first meaningful cooling can expand the feeding window without immediately relocating every fish shallow.

### Mid-Fall: Follow the Forage

As cooling becomes consistent, baitfish can become the strongest location clue on the lake.

Look for:

- coves, pockets, and creek arms holding bait;
- shallow flats immediately adjacent to deeper water;
- wind-blown points and banks;
- mouths of coves and creek arms;
- remaining green vegetation as other weeds die back;
- birds, surface activity, and visible bait;
- bait schools on sonar, especially where they intersect structure.

A bank that looked perfect yesterday can be empty today if the forage moved. In fall, do not become loyal to cover without confirming that food is still there.

### Dying Vegetation

Vegetation change can reorganize largemouth habitat quickly.

As weeds die:

- oxygen and forage value can decline locally;
- bass may leave formerly productive stretches;
- isolated healthy green clumps become more important;
- hard cover, docks, wood, rock, and the nearest depth break can replace the old weed pattern.

Fish the **best remaining vegetation**, not merely the area that had the most grass in summer.

### Late Fall: Back Toward Wintering Water

As water gets colder, bass gradually reduce the distance they roam and begin using more stable/deeper structure.

Check:

- steep rocky points;
- channel edges;
- ledges and drop-offs;
- offshore humps;
- rock piles and hard-bottom transitions;
- deeper ends of productive flats;
- bait schools near those wintering routes.

Smallmouth can group tightly on deep rock. Largemouth may also move deeper, though some remain around remaining healthy vegetation or shallow cover during mild conditions.

## Bass: How to Catch Them

### Early/Mid-Fall: Cover Water

When bass are active and forage-oriented, begin with baitfish-imitating moving presentations.

Strong choices:

- crankbait deflected off rock or hard cover;
- spinnerbait on wind-blown banks and around wood;
- chatterbait through sparse/remaining grass;
- swimbait around bait schools;
- jerkbait for suspended fish;
- [Topwater Fishing](kb://topwater-fishing) with a walking bait, buzzbait, or popper when bass are visibly feeding near the surface.

Choose lure depth to match the bait. A perfect shad color six feet above the fish is still in the wrong place.

### Search, Then Follow Up

Fall often rewards a two-step strategy:

1. Find fish with a moving lure or sonar.
2. Work the productive area again with a slower presentation.

Useful follow-ups:

- soft jerkbait/fluke when bass chase but reject hard baits;
- wacky worm around docks and shallow targets;
- Ned rig on rock/gravel;
- drop shot on deeper or suspended fish;
- jig on hard bottom or isolated cover.

This is especially effective when bass are grouped around a concentrated forage source.

### Late Fall: Lengthen the Pause

As water cools, reduce speed before abandoning productive structure.

- lengthen jerkbait pauses;
- slow-roll a small swimbait;
- drag/dead-stick a Ned rig;
- use a drop shot with minimal movement;
- crawl a finesse or football jig.

Late fall can still produce reaction bites, but the default should gradually move from **chase** toward **controlled, deliberate presentation**.

## Trout: Where to Find Them

Cooling water reverses much of the lowland-lake summer squeeze. Trout can use more of the upper water column and shoreline again as comfortable temperatures expand.

Check:

- wind-blown shorelines;
- points and cove mouths;
- creek mouths and other inflows;
- shoreline drop-offs;
- baitfish or insect activity;
- submerged wood and shoreline structure for coastal cutthroat;
- the upper water column over deeper water;
- areas recently stocked in lakes that receive fall plants.

WDFW identifies spring and fall as especially productive trout periods in many lowland lakes. Rainbow trout that spent summer deeper can return toward nearshore habitat as water cools, and resident coastal cutthroat can become more accessible after the summer deep-water period.

### Early Fall vs. Late Fall

Early fall may still require summer depth during warm afternoons. As the lake cools and mixes, trout can spread vertically.

That means the productive depth may become **less constrained**, not simply “shallower every day.” Continue using sonar, visible rises, and actual bites to determine depth.

## Trout: How to Catch Them

### Casting the Shoreline and Upper Water

When trout move higher or closer to shore:

- fan-cast a spoon or inline spinner;
- work a small crankbait/jerkbait when fish are chasing minnows;
- use long casts and natural finishes in clear water;
- use wind to identify shorelines where food is accumulating;
- count down the lure if fish are below the visible surface activity.

A fall trout may follow a lure farther than a summer fish confined to a narrow temperature band, but speed and depth still matter more than constant color changes.

### Trolling

Trolling remains effective when trout are scattered.

- Begin shallower than the mid-summer pattern when fish/sonar support it.
- Use [Inline Trolling Rig](kb://technique-inline-trolling-rig) or [Flasher Rig](kb://technique-flasher-rig).
- Make passes at several controlled depths rather than trailing an unknown amount of line.
- After a bite, repeat the same depth and speed.
- Follow bait/insect activity rather than assuming the deepest basin still holds the fish.

### Bank Fishing

Fall can reopen bank opportunities that were poor during summer heat.

Use:

- active casting with spoons/spinners to locate roaming fish;
- [Bobber Rig](kb://technique-bobber-rig) for shallow/suspended trout;
- [Slip Sinker Rig](kb://technique-slip-sinker-rig) when fish are deeper but still reachable;
- points, creek mouths, wind-blown shorelines, and drop-offs as high-percentage bank locations.

## Fall Conditions That Matter Most

### Cooling Trend

A steady cooling trend generally expands comfortable habitat for trout and encourages bass to reorganize around forage. A sudden severe cold front can temporarily reduce activity; when that happens, check the next deeper break rather than assuming fish left the area entirely.

### Wind

Fall wind can be especially productive because it combines reduced light with forage movement.

For bass:
- wind-blown points and banks;
- spinnerbait/chatterbait/crankbait opportunities;
- bait pushed into pockets or against structure.

For trout:
- insects/plankton concentrated downwind;
- upper-water feeding under chop;
- shoreline casting opportunities.

### Turnover

As surface water cools, stratified lakes can mix. During turnover, the old thermocline pattern can disappear and fish may redistribute through a wider depth range.

Do not keep fishing the summer thermocline just because it worked last month. Re-scan the water column and let current bait/fish depth establish the new pattern.

## A Practical Fall Trip Plan

1. **Find forage before committing to cover.** Surface activity, birds, bait on sonar, and panfish/minnows are major clues.
2. **Check both the summer area and the nearest shallow feeding route.** Early fall often contains both patterns.
3. **Use a moving presentation to locate bass; use controlled depth to locate trout.**
4. **Repeat productive depth and wind exposure.** Fall fish can group tightly around the same food source.
5. **As cooling progresses, shift the bass search toward wintering routes and slow the presentation.**
6. **For trout, revisit bank-accessible shoreline and upper-water habitat that was too warm in summer.**
