A broad, cupped metal spoon designed to flash, wander, and flutter as it falls like a dying or injured baitfish. Unlike a conventional casting spoon that is primarily retrieved horizontally, a flutter spoon is usually worked with a sharp upward rip followed by a controlled slack-line fall. Most strikes come on the fall.

## When and Where to Use
- Best for bass feeding on baitfish away from the bank, especially fish located with sonar.
- Target offshore points, humps, ledges, channel edges, saddles, submerged roadbeds, and other structure adjacent to deeper water.
- Particularly useful for bass suspended around or below schools of baitfish.
- A strong choice from roughly 10 ft into 30+ ft of water, depending on spoon weight and fish depth.
- Summer is the classic season as postspawn bass move offshore, but flutter spoons can also be effective in fall when bass are feeding heavily on baitfish and in winter around deeper bait schools.
- Avoid heavy vegetation and snaggy cover because the exposed treble hook fouls and snags easily.
- Use the fish finder to locate bait and bass before fishing the spoon rather than blindly covering featureless water.

## Gear
- Rod: Medium-heavy or heavy baitcasting rod with enough backbone to accelerate the spoon and drive a treble hook at depth. Around 7' to 7'8" is typical.
- Reel: Baitcasting reel.
- Line: Fluorocarbon is a good default because it sinks and provides abrasion resistance around deep structure. Braid with a fluorocarbon leader can also be used.
- For a 3/4 oz spoon, a medium-heavy baitcasting setup is appropriate. Larger 1 1/4 oz+ spoons may require a heavier rod rated for the lure weight.
- Avoid throwing oversized 2-4 oz magnum spoons unless the rod is specifically rated for them.

## Basic Cast-and-Flutter Technique
1. Cast beyond the fish or structure you want to work.
2. Let the spoon fall toward the bottom on semi-slack line. Do not keep the line tight enough to restrict its flutter.
3. Watch the line as it falls. A jump, sideways movement, or an unexpected stop can indicate a strike.
4. Once the spoon reaches bottom or the desired depth, sharply sweep or rip the rod upward about 2-4 ft.
5. Immediately lower the rod tip and give the spoon enough slack to flutter freely back down.
6. Follow the line without pulling the spoon toward you. Maintain just enough awareness to detect a strike.
7. Repeat: **rip → slack → flutter → bottom/depth → rip**.
8. Most bites occur while the spoon is falling. Reel down and set the hook when the line jumps, moves sideways, or stops unexpectedly.

The upward rip mainly resets the lure and triggers reaction; the **fall is the presentation**.

## Short-Hop Variation
When bass are holding close to bottom:
- Use shorter 1-2 ft lifts instead of large sweeps.
- Let the spoon return to bottom after each lift.
- Keep it in the strike zone rather than pulling it several feet above bottom.
- Experiment with the height and aggressiveness of the lift until the fish show a preference.

## Vertical Jigging
A flutter spoon can also be fished directly beneath the kayak:
1. Use sonar to position over or slightly upwind/up-current from the fish.
2. Drop the spoon to the depth of the fish or just above them.
3. Snap it upward.
4. Lower the rod and allow a free flutter back through the fish.
5. Watch the line closely during every fall.

This is useful for fish directly below the kayak or tightly grouped around deep structure.

## Suspended Fish
- Do not automatically drop the spoon all the way to bottom.
- If bass are suspended at 18 ft over 30 ft of water, work the spoon through approximately that depth zone.
- Count the lure down or use sonar to help keep it near the fish.
- Cast beyond a suspended school and let the spoon fall through or beside it.
- Bass often position below baitfish, making the fluttering descent especially effective.

## Size and Color
- **3-4 in / roughly 1/2-3/4 oz:** Good starting size for smallmouth and average baitfish.
- **4-5 in / roughly 3/4-1 1/4 oz:** Versatile bass size for deeper water and larger forage.
- **6 in+ / 2 oz+:** Magnum presentation for large bass feeding on large forage; requires appropriately heavy tackle.
- Start with silver, chrome, white, or natural shad finishes in clear water.
- Gold can be useful in stained water or lower light.
- Match the general size of the forage when possible, but an oversized spoon can also trigger reaction strikes.

For Lake Washington and Lake Sammamish smallmouth, a **4 in spoon around 3/4 oz in a silver/shad finish** is a practical starting point.

## Common Mistakes
- **Keeping the line too tight:** Restricts the spoon and kills the flutter.
- **Fishing only the rip:** The fall is normally the strike-producing part of the retrieve.
- **Ignoring the line:** Many strikes happen on slack line and may only appear as unusual line movement.
- **Using too much lure for the rod:** Magnum spoons require much heavier tackle than ordinary bass lures.
- **Fishing heavy vegetation:** The treble hook readily collects weeds.
- **Blind casting featureless water:** The technique is strongest when sonar has identified bait, bass, or promising offshore structure.
- **Dropping below suspended fish:** Keep the lure in the depth zone the fish are using.

## Kayak Strategy
- Use sonar to search offshore structure rather than continuously casting while moving.
- When bait and bass appear, stop and position the kayak so the spoon can fall naturally through the fish.
- Wind or current can quickly move a kayak off the target; reposition often.
- A cast-and-flutter presentation covers a point, hump, or break faster than a bottom-contact finesse rig.
- If fish become concentrated directly beneath the kayak, switch from casting to vertical jigging.
- Because strikes often happen on slack line, keep the rod positioned so you can quickly reel down and load the rod when the line moves.
